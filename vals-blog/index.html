Models don’t know what they’re good at

Anand Shah, Andrey Fradkin, Rohit Krishnan · MarketBench, with Vals AI · draft of 2026-10-19 · click a benchmark chip under any figure to subset it

We usually talk about capabilities as isolated to individual models. A lab releases a model, describes its capabilities in a model card, and evals help us compare and track these capabilities across models. Yet, increasingly, the capabilities we want to measure belong to systems of models: a swarm of agents working in concert, an orchestrator coordinating many subagents, or even just a router allocating tasks to distinct models. Unfortunately, evals over systems of agents are expensive and our conceptual vocabulary for understanding model coordination is extremely limited.

This post measures one bottleneck to modeling coordination: self-knowledge. Decentralized coordination requires models to have some degree of self-knowledge -- the ability to reliably answer questions like "how much will this cost to run?" or "can you handle this task?". This would be expensive to test from scratch, as we'd have to first produce model predictions across tasks then also run inference against those tasks to test outcomes.

But since Vals AI has already conducted benchmarking runs, we are able to ask models to predict their own success and compare them to a pre-computed ground truth. We ask 18 models from nine labs to predict their own success and cost on seven benchmarks, and score the predictions against Vals AI's production outcomes on the same tasks. We find that i) models overwhelmingly don't know their own capabilities, ii) that the direction of miscalibration seems to be a persistent property of the model's family, iii) that the obvious fixes (more thinking, revealing information to the model on its capabilities) don't meaningfully improve self-knowledge, iv) that the bitter lesson has not come for self-knowledge: models don't improve along self-knowledge as they become more capable. Finally, we show that left to coordinate on their own, models do worse than random, but that a router with perfect self-knowledge produces enormous gains. On the tasks in the Vals Index, the best single model solves 46%; a router that allocates along model self-reports solves 39.5%, worse than random; and a router with perfect self-knowledge would solve 67% of tasks in the bench set, at a fifth of the cost.

Setup

We asked each model to predict its own performance on tasks from seven benchmarks: Finance Agent (financial analysis), EMB (Excel modeling), Legal Research, Vibe Code Bench, Code Migration, and Terminal-Bench 2.1 and 4.0. Six of these are components of the Vals Index, so we can set our results against Index scores later. We test fifteen models from nine labs: OpenAI (GPT-6 Astra, Sol, Luna), Anthropic (Fable 5.1, Sonnet 5), Google (Gemini 4 Argon, Gemini 3.8 Flash), Meta (Muse Spark 1.3), xAI (Grok 4.7, 4.6), DeepSeek (V4 Pro, V4.1 Flash), Alibaba (Qwen 3.8 Max), Moonshot (Kimi K3) and Z.ai (GLM 5.3). Every result below uses all 719 tasks across the benchmarks, unless stated otherwise.

A prediction, which we call a bid, has two parts: the probability the model will pass the task, and the cost in dollars it expects to spend. The model sees only the task description before bidding, and is not given any additional files, tools, internet access, or a chance to attempt the task before bidding. We then score the bid against the realized outcome from when Vals’ production eval data. A pass is full completion of the task.

Bids are 2x1 vectors, so we score the two components separately and together. First, for each component, we report the mean squared error between the predicted and realized outcome (importantly, for the predicted probability of passing the task, this is a Brier score). Second, to put a dollar figure on a model’s (lack of) self-knowledge, we have each model play a single-player auction with their bid. On each task the model faces a reserve price drawn at random; it wins the task when its bid clears the reserve, then runs it, and earns $100 if it passes minus what it actually spent. The auction is incentive compatible, so it’s optimal for a model to bid its true chance and true cost. The outcome we report here is the gap between what a model earned on its own bids and what it would have earned bidding its true realized chance and cost, in dollars per task.

Results

Across the 719 tasks all 15 models bid on, the best model at predicting its own outcomes is Kimi K3 (with a Brier of 0.177), while GLM 5.3 and Qwen 3.8 Max are next best on average and statistically indistinguishable. At the bottom is GPT-6 Luna (with an average Brier score of 0.251). The null prediction (always guessing 50% probability of passing a task) corresponds to a Brier score of 0.25.

There are no models that demonstrate exceptionally good or exceptionally bad self-knowledge. All the models tested sit between 0.177 and 0.251, and the average is 0.205. Only about 0.01 of that is outcome noise (a task with a true pass rate of 40% contributes 0.24 even to a perfect forecaster, and most tasks here are near 0 or 1), so nearly all of the error is in the models' beliefs.

However, models do have substantial heterogeneity in the direction of their miscalibration. Pooling all models, the average bid is 43% and the average success rate is 41%, which looks calibrated. That is because the overbidders and underbidders cancel. Seven models overstate their chances by 10 to 21 points: GPT-6 Luna bids 59% and succeeds 38% of the time, GPT-6 Astra bids 65% and succeeds 46%. Five understate by 7 to 17 points: Sonnet 5 bids 24% and succeeds 40%, Fable 5.1 bids 34% and succeeds 47%. Only three models (Kimi K3, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Grok 4.7) are within 5 points of their own success rate.

Mistakes run in the family

We regress each model's gap on its lab and the benchmark, with benchmark fixed effects and standard errors clustered by task. Looking at model families, OpenAI models are generally overconfident, bidding 17 points above the average model and Anthropic models are generally underconfident, bidding 16 points below (both with standard errors near 1.5). Meta, Z.ai and Alibaba also sell themselves short, by 8 to 11 points, while Google and xAI run a few points over. The lab alone explains 29% of the variation in gaps across models and benchmarks; lab and benchmark together explain 70%.

<Exhibit 3: family_gap.png, one panel per benchmark, models ordered by gap, family means beside them>

However, there are also large benchmark-specific effects. On the Excel modeling benchmark, every family is overconfident, and on legal research most families are (the average gaps are +14 and +12 points); meanwhile, on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and Vibe Code every family underbids (−16 and −11). Only OpenAI's models sit at or near the overbidding end of each ranking regardless of the benchmark. As such, averaging across benchmarks cancels these errors and makes every model look better calibrated than it is on any one of them. Gemini 3.8 Flash bids 72% on legal research, where it succeeds 40% of the time, and 63% on Terminal-Bench, where it succeeds 87%; while pooled, it looks deceptively well calibrated.

No easy ways to improve these capabilities

We run two ablations to try and understand what self-knowledge capabilities are sensitive to.

The first is thinking. Our baseline bids already let models reason at a high effort setting, so we asked what happens with less: dropping each model to its lowest effort makes the bids worse by 0.013 Brier (interval 0.008 to 0.018), i.e. thinking helps, but by less than the noise between two runs of the same model. Going the other way, from high to the maximum setting on Opus 5.5, changes little on the 40 tasks where the setting actually moved (−0.03, a small improvement). In general, self-knowledge seems inelastic to thinking effort.

The second is to show the model its own track record, i.e. its past success rate on the benchmark. This helps more, by 0.022 Brier on average, and a lot for some models (GPT-6 Astra improves by 0.068, Argon by 0.040). But a bid equal to the historical rate itself does exactly as well: the difference between "model given the rate" and "the rate" is −0.002, with an interval that includes zero. Models do move their bids away from the rate they are shown, by 5 to 12 points on a typical task, but those adjustments add nothing on net. Whatever the model adds about the task in front of it is noise.

More effort at execution doesn't do it either. Muse 1.3 Max beats Muse 1.3 by 8 points on Terminal-Bench and 15 on legal research, and is no better calibrated overall. Self-knowledge is a separate capability from ability, and as far as we can tell nobody is training for it.

The bitter lesson is nowhere to be found

Self-knowledge does not ride the wave of general capabilities. The models that score highest on the Vals Index are no better at predicting themselves than the ones that score lowest.

Further, even within families, more capable models don’t seem to exhibit more self-knowledge than their predecessors. E.g., Gemini 4 Argon improves on Gemini 3.8 Flash by 14 points on the Vals index yet predicts itself slightly worse (Brier 0.202 vs 0.198).

<Exhibit 5: emergence.png, Vals Index score against Brier, one point per model, arrows from older to newer within family, dashed line at 0.25>

Interestingly, there are also large benchmark-specific effects when interacting with model capabilities, suggesting self-knowledge is different from most capabilities altogether. For example, on Terminal-Bench 4.0, newer (more capable) models are worse at predicting themselves than older (less capable) ones. Argon's Brier is 0.13 higher than Flash's, Muse Max's is 0.06 higher than Muse 1.3's, and Opus 5.5's is 0.24 higher than Sonnet 5's. To further investigate within family trends, we further supplement with three earlier models (Opus 5, Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol), and find that self-knowledge in recent models is not increasing even relative to these earlier models.

Value being left on the table

Since we observe outcomes, we can compute what each model would have earned in the auction had it bid its actual success rate and cost on every task, and compare that to what it earns bidding on its own. On average, from their own bids, models earn $6.64 per task. Bidding truthfully, they would earn $16.90 per task. Every model is leaving money on the table.

<Exhibit: fig_oracle.png>

The cost is larger when models have to coordinate. Suppose a router receives a bid from each of the 14 models on every task and assigns the task to the model whose bid implies the highest expected profit. This is the simplest decentralized system to coordinate models to tasks: each model reports what it’s willing to bid for tasks, and the router runs an auction to produce a matching between models and tasks. That system solves 39.5% of tasks at $5.39 per task, which does worse than random – assigning tasks at random across all our models solves 42% at $4.66 per task. A router with perfect self-knowledge, which assigns each task to the model that in fact does best on it, solves 67% at $0.92 per task.

Conclusion

We asked 18 models to predict their own success and cost on real tasks and scored the predictions against what happened. The predictions are poor. The best model is only modestly better than always saying 50%, and the worst is worse than random. Giving models more time to think, or their own track record, barely moves the predictions, and more capable models do not emergently seem to improve in self-knowledge (and in some cases, even get worse).

Self-knowledge seems to be a capability distinct from the ones most labs and evals measure. Yet, as useful work is increasingly done by many models working in concert, it may become an important bottleneck to coordination.
